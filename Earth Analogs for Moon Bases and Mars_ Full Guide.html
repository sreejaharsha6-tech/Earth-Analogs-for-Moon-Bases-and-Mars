Earth Analogs for Moon Bases and Mars

NASA Space Apps 2026 challenge: Identify Earth locations that are analogs of the permanent Moon base locations and Mars. This guide explains the two target worlds, profiles 12 Earth analog sites in depth, scores how well each one matches, and recommends the best site for each kind of test.

Summary

Short answer. No Earth place copies the Moon or Mars, so analogs are chosen feature by feature. For the lunar south pole the best matches are polar sites with low sun and permafrost (Devon Island, Svalbard, Antarctica) plus volcanic basalt for regolith and lava-tube work (Hawaii, Flagstaff, Lanzarote, Iceland). For Mars the best matches are cold, dry, mineral-rich deserts (Atacama, Dry Valleys, Utah, Rio Tinto, Uyuni). Devon Island and the Dry Valleys score well for both.

Why analog sites matter

A planetary analog is a place on Earth that shares geology, climate, terrain or operating conditions with another world. They let teams test rovers, drills, habitats, spacesuits, communications and procedures before spending money on launches. Apollo crews practiced geology in Iceland, Arizona and Idaho. Today NASA field teams work in deserts, polar regions, volcanic fields and under the sea, and run mission simulations such as HERA, a three-story habitat at Johnson Space Center, and a planned yearlong Moon and Mars Exploration Analog.

There are two kinds: environmental analogs (the ground and climate look like the target) and operational analogs (crews and equipment practice mission routines). This project focuses on environmental matches, with notes on operations.

The Moon base target: lunar south pole

NASA’s Artemis plans center on a sustained base near the lunar south pole, where astronauts could live and work. The Moon’s axis is tilted only about 1.5°, so the Sun never rises more than a degree or two above the horizon at the pole. Some ridges receive sunlight for most of the year, which is good for solar power, while the floors of deep craters have not seen sunlight for billions of years and are among the coldest places measured in the solar system, near −250 °C. Those permanently shadowed regions may hold water ice, which could supply drinking water, oxygen and rocket propellant.

Candidate Artemis landing regions studied around the pole include the rims of Shackleton, de Gerlache, Nobile, Faustini and Haworth craters, the Connecting Ridge between Shackleton and de Gerlache, and Malapert Massif. Common hazards: steep slopes, boulders, long black shadows, abrasive electrostatic dust, extreme cold, radiation (no atmosphere or magnetic field), and 1/6 gravity.

What an Earth analog must supply: rugged cratered terrain, basaltic or glassy regolith, extreme cold, low-angle sunlight with long shadows, ice in soil or permafrost, lava tubes for shelter, and isolation.

The Mars target

Mars has a thin carbon-dioxide atmosphere (about 0.6% of Earth’s surface pressure), an average temperature near −60 °C, and a day (sol) of 24 h 37 min. Its surface is basaltic rock under iron-rich red dust containing perchlorate salts, which are toxic to humans and may harm equipment. Water exists as polar ice caps, buried ice at mid-latitudes and water bound in minerals. Ancient lakes and river deltas are the prime targets for finding past life: Jezero crater (Perseverance) holds a delta with carbonate minerals, and Gale crater (Curiosity) holds layered clay and sulfate deposits on Mount Sharp.

Mars’s big operational challenge is distance: radio signals take about 4 to 24 minutes one way, a crew cannot be rescued quickly, and the trip takes 6 to 9 months. So Mars analogs emphasize autonomy, long isolation, in-situ resource use and mineral-rich terrain.

What an Earth analog must supply: cold hyper-arid desert, oxidized iron soils, evaporite salts and clays, volcanic basalt, ancient water-carved landforms, and remote isolation.

The 8 matching features explained

1. Terrain

Slope, roughness, craters and boulders. Decides rover mobility, landing safety and construction effort.

Moon: Cratered with steep walls and boulders. Mars: Dunes, rocky plains, canyons.

How to measure: LOLA/MOLA vs SRTM slope. Best analogs: Devon Island, Teide, Flagstaff.

2. Dryness

How little liquid water and humidity the site has. Affects soil chemistry, dust behavior, corrosion and life-detection tests.

Moon: Bone dry. Mars: Cold hyper-arid desert.

How to measure: SMAP, GPM, climate records. Best analogs: Atacama, Dry Valleys, Death Valley.

3. Temperature swing

Daily and seasonal temperature range. Tests batteries, seals, lubricants, electronics and suits.

Moon: Extreme: −250 °C shadows to warm lit ridges. Mars: −125 to +20 °C per sol.

How to measure: Diviner vs MODIS/ECOSTRESS. Best analogs: Devon Island, Dry Valleys, Svalbard.

4. Basalt

Volcanic rock and basaltic soils. Sets the right soil for ISRU, digging and 3D-printed construction.

Moon: Basalt maria and glassy regolith. Mars: Basaltic crust with iron-rich dust.

How to measure: Mineral indices, geology maps. Best analogs: Hawaii, Flagstaff, Iceland, Teide.

5. Ice

Ground ice and permafrost. Drives water mining, drilling and propellant tests.

Moon: Likely in shadowed craters. Mars: Polar and buried mid-latitude ice.

How to measure: Mini-RF radar vs permafrost maps. Best analogs: Devon Island, Dry Valleys, Svalbard.

6. Polar light

Low-angle sun and long shadows. Shapes solar power, thermal design and navigation in glare and darkness.

Moon: Sun skims the horizon; some ridges near-constantly lit. Mars: Not a Mars feature.

How to measure: Illumination models vs sun geometry. Best analogs: Svalbard, Devon Island.

7. Isolation

Remoteness and rescue delay. Tests autonomy, crew psychology and emergency plans.

Moon: Days from home. Mars: Months from home.

How to measure: Distance to roads and towns. Best analogs: Devon Island, Dry Valleys, Atacama.

8. Access

How reachable the site is for real test campaigns. Cost and logistics decide whether an analog is practical.

Moon: Reachable. Mars: Reachable.

How to measure: Roads, ports, airports. Best analogs: Hawaii, Utah, Teide, Iceland.

Atacama Desert, Chile

Analog for: Mars Northern Chile, about 24° S

Moon 57% · Mars 75%

Why: The driest non-polar desert on Earth; some spots record almost no rain for years. Oxidized soil, nitrate and perchlorate salts and very low microbe counts make it Mars-like.

Evidence of use: Carnegie Mellon’s Zoë rover tested life-detection methods here, and NASA teams study its soil and microbes.

Limits: Has air, 1 g, no ice or polar lighting.

How to compute real scores (method)

  1. Define target fingerprints from LRO LOLA slopes and Diviner temperatures (Moon) and MOLA, HiRISE and CRISM (Mars).
  2. Build Earth feature layers on a global grid: SRTM/ASTER slope and roughness, MODIS land surface temperature range, SMAP and GPM dryness, Landsat/Sentinel-2/ASTER mineral indices, NASA POWER sunlight geometry, permafrost maps, distance to roads.
  3. Score and rank each cell with a weighted similarity (cosine distance or random forest).
  4. Validate that known analogs (Atacama, Devon, Dry Valleys) rank near the top.
  5. Report new candidates and add an accessibility and safety score for real campaigns.

Limits: every analog has air, 1 g and biology, so dust, radiation and low gravity need simulants, drop towers or parabolic flights. Our current scores are estimates until computed from data.

Data portals: pds.nasa.gov (Moon and Mars), earthdata.nasa.gov (Earth), astrogeology.usgs.gov (planetary maps).

Glossary

Analog: an Earth place or mission that imitates conditions elsewhere. Regolith: loose dusty rock soil covering the Moon or Mars. PSR: permanently shadowed region, a crater floor that never sees the Sun. ISRU: in-situ resource utilization, making water, oxygen or fuel from local materials. EVA: extravehicular activity, a spacewalk. Simulant: Earth material made to mimic lunar or Martian soil. Perchlorate: toxic salt found in Martian soil. Lava tube: hollow tunnel left by flowing lava.
